THE SCIENCE BEHIND THE VIEW

A model of what we know.

Earth, the Solar System, the measured stars, the Milky Way, and neighboring galaxies occupy one continuous three-dimensional world. Scroll or use the zoom slider to travel across scales. Destination buttons move the camera through that same space; you can interrupt a journey by dragging, scrolling, or pressing Escape.

Solar System

Planet and Moon positions are computed for the selected UTC date using Astronomy Engine 2.1.19, a VSOP87-derived planetary and lunar ephemeris tested by its author against JPL Horizons and NOVAS, with a design target of about one arcminute. The atlas additionally tests representative dates against independent JPL Horizons vectors. Earth is its actual center, not the Earth–Moon barycenter. Positions are geometric, in the J2000 reference frame: light travel time, aberration, and a particular observer’s atmospheric refraction are not applied. This is a coordinate atlas, not a simulated telescope exposure.

Physical mean radii are the default. Turn off True sizes to enlarge distant planet disks for visibility. Orbits are guides: planetary ellipses use JPL’s approximate Keplerian elements for 1800–2050, while the Moon’s guide samples its ephemeris over a sidereal month. Planet and Moon poles and prime-meridian rotation follow Astronomy Engine’s implementation of the IAU 2015 model. Surface maps are static composite imagery, not current weather; their absolute longitude registration and color are not independently calibrated. Spherical surfaces omit oblateness and terrain. The optional asteroid belt is a statistical illustration, not measured asteroid positions. Other moons, dwarf planets, and comets are omitted.

Stars we have measured

109,400 stars from David Nash’s HYG 4.1 catalog, combining Hipparcos, Yale, and Gliese catalogs. Positions are at epoch J2000.0, transformed into Galactic coordinates. This subset includes catalog distances up to 1,000 parsecs (3,262 light-years), excluding missing-distance sentinels. It is incomplete and biased toward brighter stars; distance estimates have varying uncertainty. Proper motion is not animated.

958 named, bright, or nearby entries are searchable. Multiple stars can remain unresolved at this scale. Point sizes, brightness, and color are adapted for visibility; they do not represent stellar radii or calibrated exposure. Exoplanet orbits are not included in this atlas.

The Milky Way

The default view shows the HYG catalog and the Galactic center marker. HYG’s 1,000-parsec distance cut is a survey selection; the empty space outside it is not an observed absence of stars. We do not have a complete 3D stellar census or an external photograph of our galaxy. The Illustrative Milky Way option adds an explicitly schematic density model of the disk, bar, and arms. Its generated particles are not individual observed stars, and its arm paths and outer boundary are not measurement results.

The center uses the J2000 radio position of Sagittarius A* and an adopted Sun-to-center distance of 8,277 pc from GRAVITY Collaboration (2022), with reported statistical uncertainty of 9 pc and systematics of roughly 30 pc. This is an estimate, not an exact known coordinate.

The Local Group

Ten selected neighboring galaxies use the Local Volume Database v1.1.1 (August 12, 2026), compiled by Andrew B. Pace with references to each original study. This is a pinned selection, not a complete census or an assertion that every entry is the uniquely best distance measurement. Distances are inferred from observations and can differ between methods. We retain their uncertainty and identify the adopted study when you select a galaxy. The Magellanic Clouds use eclipsing-binary distances from Pietrzyński et al. (2019) and Graczyk et al. (2020); statistical and systematic errors are both included.

Blue outlines with soft shading show sky-projected photometric contours in the observer’s tangent plane, not 3D disks. Most use the catalog’s half-light semi-major axis, ellipticity, and position angle. Andromeda and Triangulum use the RC3 B-band 25 mag/arcsec² isophote; those contours are not directly comparable to half-light ellipses. Fainter stellar halos can extend much farther. Where the source lacks a complete ellipse, only a center marker is drawn. These measured overlays do not infer disk inclinations, far-side structure, or individual stars. The Galaxy portraits option adds enlarged, screen-facing illustrations at the catalog centers in the overview. Their spirals, dust lanes, inclinations, and colors are artistic map symbols, not photographs or measurements. They fade away on approach so the projected measurements remain available for inspection.

Gold line segments show each adopted distance interval along our line of sight, corresponding to reported 1σ bounds; they are not the physical length of the galaxy. Marker size, color, halos, and contour shading are map styling, not photometry or physical size. Distance rings are 1, 2, and 3 million light-years from the Milky Way center. The dashed ruler between the Milky Way and Andromeda reports the three-dimensional separation of their adopted centers, not an orbit or trajectory. The overview anchor is the framing midpoint of the Milky Way and Andromeda, not a measured center of mass. Stars stay at the catalog epoch and galaxy positions stay at their adopted observed estimates; the planetary clock does not propagate them, or reconstruct a simultaneous present-day universe from ancient light. Galaxy motions, interactions, dark matter, and expansion are not simulated.
